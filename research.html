My research has evolved from an initial interest in geography and urbanization toward broader questions about how the state transforms itself and society in the course of development, as well as how political economies evolve under conditions of a strong state and a weak society. In general, my earlier and current research spans three areas: political economy of China, local governance, and state capacity.

Political Economy of China

Campaign Mobilization and Political Selection under Personalized Power

Under Review

Abstract

How do authoritarian leaders mobilize, evaluate, and select subordinates while weakening power-sharing arrangements and suppressing elites? This article argues that campaigns, traditionally viewed as extraordinary governance instruments for overcoming implementation gaps, also serve as mechanisms of political selection. Leveraging a unique dataset documenting the career trajectories, performance records, and patronage linkages of over eight thousand county-level officials since 2003, I evaluate this argument in the context of China’s one-party regime. My analysis shows that both local patronage ties and conventional economic performance metrics that previously predicted political advancement have lost their significance following the consolidation of personalized power. Instead, political selection has become increasingly tied to officials' demonstrated capacity to implement leader-prioritized objectives during campaigns. This article advances the debate on competence and loyalty in authoritarian political selection while shedding new light on the governance consequences of personalization.

Strategic Accountability in Hierarchy: Disasters, Sanctions, and Cadre Careers in China

Under Review

Abstract

How does accountability operate when citizens cannot remove officials through elections? I develop a theory of strategic accountability in authoritarian hierarchies, in which rulers use hierarchical structures to impose governance accountability on subnational agents while retaining discretion over who bears responsibility and how far sanctions extend. The theory predicts that such accountability is substantive rather than purely rhetorical, rises with social damage, concentrates among lower-level officials, and produces inconsistent career impacts. I test these hypotheses using a combination of an original dataset on accountability following technological disasters in China from 2005 to 2024, a harmonized cross-national benchmark, and a unique official-level dataset on the career trajectories of Chinese county-level officials. Taken together, the findings identify a substantive but politically controlled form of accountability distinct from elections, responsiveness, and informal social discipline.

Local Governance

Who Partners with Whom under Hierarchy? Rethinking Partner Selection in Interjurisdictional Cooperation

with Xinhui Jiang · Revise and Resubmit

Abstract

Scholarship on interjurisdictional cooperation has long debated whether geographic proximity and socioeconomic similarity facilitate or impede cooperation, with inconclusive empirical findings. This article advances this debate by incorporating the role of hierarchical intervention in cooperation formation. Drawing on an original dataset on inter-county cooperation in China (2009–2020) and fieldwork in 24 localities, we show that patterns of partner selection vary systematically with the degree of hierarchical intervention. Arranged cooperation, coordinated top-down, links socioeconomically dissimilar jurisdictions. Mandate-induced cooperation, driven by shared policy mandates, tends to emerge among geographically proximate and similar localities. Self-initiated cooperation also relies on socioeconomic similarity, while strategically avoiding nearby political competitors. We illustrate these dynamics through regression results and three case studies. Taken together, the findings offer a revised framework for understanding interjurisdictional cooperation under hierarchy.

The Price of Losing Autonomy: Assessing the Economic Impact of County-to-District Mergers in China

Urban Affairs Review, 60(6), 1839–1870, 2024. DOI

Abstract

Amalgamations in democratic settings often result in disadvantages for small and peripheral units, as their political power diminishes in the merged jurisdictions. This article extends the discussion to China by assessing the economic outcomes of county-level units after their conversion into districts directly controlled by city authorities. Leveraging a large county-year panel dataset and employing an advanced causal effect estimator, my analysis reveals a generally less optimistic outlook for these county-turned districts following the mergers. The negative impacts are initially evident in microeconomic indicators, especially in resident deposits, and later materialize in the long-term trajectories of macroeconomic indicators. Furthermore, heterogeneous analysis and a comparative case study in Hangzhou city link the problem to the loss of administrative autonomy. Notably, the negative impacts are less likely to manifest in county-turned districts that are under provincial protection and therefore maintain a certain degree of administrative autonomy after the mergers.

The Multilevel Politics of County-to-District Mergers in China

with Kyle Jaros · Journal of Contemporary China, 32(144), 932–950, 2023. DOI

Abstract

County-to-district mergers (chexian gaiqu), a major form of “administrative division adjustment” (ADA), have occurred with striking frequency across Chinese cities in recent decades. Despite the high stakes of county mergers, scholars have yet to systematically analyze the political variables that shape where and when such reforms occur. Addressing this gap, the authors assemble a novel dataset to examine the relationship between institutional and leadership factors at different government levels and the incidence of county mergers. This analysis highlights the importance of national leadership priorities in shaping the frequency and regional distribution of county mergers. It also finds that, at least in times of greater central policy permissiveness, the bargaining power of cities is a strong predictor of which localities carry out county mergers.

Colonial Legacy, Private Property, and Rural Development: Evidence from Namibian Countryside

with Vladimir Chlouba · Economic History of Developing Regions, 36(1), 30–56, 2021. DOI

Abstract

Does the legacy of direct colonial rule, through its impact on property rights security, affect rural development in Africa? Although mainstream economic theory links secure property rights to development, extant micro-level evidence from the continent remains mixed. We take advantage of a natural experiment in Namibia, exploiting as-if random application of direct colonial rule that later affected property rights security. Using detailed census data and matching on underlying climatic conditions, we find evidence of more commercialized agricultural cultivation in directly ruled areas. We relate this finding to differing tenure regimes. In formerly indirectly ruled areas where land is still allocated by traditional elites, own-account agricultural activity for the market and living standards lag behind formerly directly ruled regions. Our work has direct implications for students of colonial legacies and land tenure regimes.

Subnational Territorial Reforms and State Capacity: Evidence from the Developing World

Global Public Policy and Governance, 2(2), 232–251, 2022. DOI

Abstract

Governments often change their internal territorial divisions to pursue either political or governance goals. Over the recent decades, territorial reforms of splitting local government units, known as government fragmentation, have gained popularity in the developing world, and further stimulate the ongoing discussion about jurisdiction size and the quality of government. Building on this stream of literature, this paper seeks to empirically examine how the design of the primary administrative division affects the overall capacity of the state to implement its policies. Drawing on a country-year panel of 132 developing countries in the 1960–2012 period, the paper documents an inverse U-shaped relationship between the degree of government fragmentation and state capacity measured by different indicators, which suggests a tradeoff in designing administrative divisions. Furthermore, the curvilinear relationship is found more pronounced in countries under a unitary government system or with a high level of ethnic fractionalization.
